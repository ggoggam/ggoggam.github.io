The Gershgorin circle theorem tells us where a square matrix's eigenvalues can lie without having to calculate them.
I first learned it in college, and it has been one of my favorite theorems since. Something reminded me of it today.

### Theorem

For each row of a complex square matrix $A$, draw a closed disk in the complex plane centered at $a_{ii}$ with radius $R_i = \sum_{j \neq i} | a_{ij} |$, where $a_{ij}$ are the matrix entries. The radius is the sum of the absolute values of the other entries in that row.
Every eigenvalue of $A$ lies in at least one of these Gershgorin circles.

### Proof

Let $\lambda$ be an eigenvalue of $A$ and $\mathbf{x}$ its corresponding eigenvector.
Choose the largest entry of the eigenvector in absolute value: $i = \arg \max_{k} | x_k |$.
The definition of an eigenvalue gives $\sum_{j \neq i} a_{ij} x_j = (\lambda - a_{ii}) x_i$.
Apply the triangle inequality and use $| x_j | / |x_i| \leq 1$:

$$
|\lambda - a_{ii}| = \left| \sum_{j \neq i} \frac{a_{ij} x_j}{x_i} \right| \leq \sum_{j \neq i} \left| a_{ij} \right| \frac{\left| x_j \right|}{\left| x_i \right|} \leq \sum_{j \neq i} \left| a_{ij} \right|.
$$
